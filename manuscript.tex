\title{Correctness and Predictive Uncertainty in Bayesian TSK Fuzzy Regression: A Reproducible Evaluation of Spike-and-Slab Model Averaging}

\author[addr]{Zhiren Xiao~\orcid{0009-0008-2164-4557}}
\address[addr]{Guangdong University of Finance, Guangzhou, China}

\begin{abstract}
Reliable uncertainty estimates from fuzzy regressions require both a correctly specified implementation and a comparison protocol that exposes the cost of model uncertainty.
We revisit a first-order Takagi--Sugeno--Kang (TSK) regression implementation and rebuild its dense, conjugate Bayesian, BIC-based, and rule-level spike-and-slab estimators from corrected code.
The correction freezes the Gaussian membership partition at training time, uses a scaled conjugate prior, evaluates firing strengths in the log domain, and reports direct posterior-predictive quantiles for Bayesian methods.
Across 30 outer 80/20 splits on three targets from two UCI regression datasets, corrected TSK-LS obtains $R^2=0.957$, $0.923$, and $0.786$ on Energy-Heating, Energy-Cooling, and Concrete, respectively; rule-level Gibbs sampling with Bayesian model averaging obtains $0.957$, $0.922$, and $0.784$.
The corresponding Gibbs prediction-interval coverage is $0.940$, $0.934$, and $0.938$ at a nominal 95\%, while the BIC-based comparator gives similar performance under the rebuilt protocol rather than the collapse reported in the earlier implementation.
Gaussian-process references have higher point-prediction accuracy and narrower intervals on these benchmarks, and split-conformal TSK intervals are wider but achieve higher empirical coverage.
The resulting evidence supports a narrower conclusion: spike-and-slab model averaging is a reproducible implementation option, but it does not provide a demonstrated accuracy or calibration advantage here.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}

Predictive models in engineering increasingly need more than point estimates: designers require uncertainty estimates that remain interpretable and reproducible under limited data.
Rule-based regressions with linear consequents---of which Takagi--Sugeno--Kang (TSK) fuzzy systems \cite{takagi1985fuzzy} are a canonical instance---are attractive here: they combine nonlinear approximation with a compact, interpretable architecture.
Their linear consequents also make Bayesian treatment especially natural, yielding posterior distributions, prediction intervals, and principled model selection. Building-energy simulation exemplifies the data regime that motivates this work: each simulation run is computationally expensive, yielding training sets of only hundreds of samples \cite{tsanas2012accurate}.

The methodological question addressed here is narrower than a general claim about sparse fuzzy regression.
Spike-and-slab priors \cite{mitchell1988bayesian,george1993variable} offer a way to represent uncertain rule activation, while Bayesian TSK models and prediction-interval methods already provide several probabilistic alternatives \cite{gu2017bayesian,gu2018bayesian,liu2017bayesianzero,miskony2018predictionintervals}.
The practical question is whether a rule-level Gibbs/BMA implementation changes predictive accuracy or uncertainty after the fuzzy partition, prior parameterization, and numerical linear algebra have been made explicit.

This question is also an implementation question.
The spreads of Gaussian membership functions define the fuzzy partition and should be estimated from training data and reused at inference.
Recomputing them from query data can change the design matrix after fitting.
We therefore reproduce this failure mode as an audit, but do not use its unstable output as a corrected-versus-buggy effect size.
All headline comparisons use the frozen-partition implementation and are regenerated from the same code.

We use a block-Gibbs sampler for rule-level spike-and-slab coefficients and predict by Bayesian model averaging (BMA), so posterior draws retain both within-model parameter variation and between-configuration variation.
We compare this estimator with dense TSK least squares, a dense conjugate posterior, the BIC-based thresholded comparator retained for continuity, Gaussian-process and support-vector references, and a split-conformal TSK interval.
The primary contribution is an evidence-led evaluation of this pipeline, including correctness gates, proper probabilistic scores, and an explicit account of remaining MCMC mixing risk.

Our contributions are threefold:
\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Implementation correctness and traceability.} We make the FCM exponent, frozen membership spreads, no-intercept design, scaled prior, stable covariance calculation, log-domain firing strengths, and direct predictive quantiles explicit, and test them with small-\(R\) analytic and enumeration checks.
\item \textbf{Rebuilt predictive comparison.} We regenerate 30-split results and report point metrics, coverage, interval width, WIS, and CRPS for the TSK, Gaussian-process, and conformal comparators. The rebuilt evidence does not support a claim that Gibbs/BMA dominates the dense or conjugate TSK baselines.
\item \textbf{Scope of sparse selection.} We examine rule-level and coefficient-level sparsity in low-dimensional benchmarks, a controlled irrelevant-feature probe, and a real high-dimensional probe. The results describe the tested regime; they do not establish a universal sparsity boundary.
\end{enumerate}

Section~\ref{sec:related} reviews related work. Section~\ref{sec:method} presents the TSK model, the membership bug, and the Gibbs-based Bayesian estimators. Section~\ref{sec:setup} describes the experimental setup. Section~\ref{sec:results} reports the corrected baseline, the main comparison, calibration, and the sparsity boundary. Section~\ref{sec:discussion} discusses implications and limitations, and Section~\ref{sec:conclusion} concludes.

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}

\subsection{Bayesian TSK Fuzzy Systems}

Bayesian TSK research includes probabilistic classifiers \cite{gu2017bayesian}, joint structure and parameter learning \cite{gu2018bayesian}, zero-order regression models \cite{liu2017bayesianzero}, and Bayesian calibration of computer models \cite{wang2021bayesian}.
Prediction intervals have also been constructed for adaptive neuro-fuzzy systems and evolving fuzzy systems \cite{miskony2018predictionintervals,cartagena2024evolving}.
These studies motivate the dense conjugate posterior used here as a reference, while the present evaluation asks how an explicit rule-level spike-and-slab layer behaves under the same TSK representation.

\subsection{Sparse TSK and Bayesian Variable Selection}

On the frequentist side, sparse TSK systems use embedded feature selection, group regularization, or rule extraction \cite{kbs2024embedded,zhang2023sparse,neuralnet2024sparsetsk,xue2023dgaletsk}.
Recent high-dimensional TSK work also explores multi-head representations \cite{bian2025mhtsk}.
These approaches and the present rule-level prior answer different questions: they emphasize point prediction, feature selection, or architecture, whereas this paper evaluates posterior model averaging for uncertainty.
Spike-and-slab priors and Bayesian model averaging have a broad methodological literature \cite{mitchell1988bayesian,george1993variable,ishwaran2005spike,hoeting1999bayesian}.
Information-criterion and Laplace approximations remain useful comparators, but their reliability is problem-dependent \cite{gelfand1994bayesian,chipman2001practical,kasprzak2025laplace}.
Den{\oe}ux's ENNreg \cite{denoeux2023ennreg} provides a belief-function alternative to probabilistic fuzzy regression with prediction intervals.
Distribution-free alternatives such as \emph{split} conformal prediction \cite{vovk2005algorithmic,lei2018distributionfree} provide finite-sample coverage without posterior sampling.
They require a separate calibration split and do not provide the posterior-predictive decomposition used by BMA; we include split conformal as a complementary, not interchangeable, reference.

\subsection{Reproducibility in Soft Computing}

Reproducibility failures arising from silent implementation errors are a recognized concern in applied machine learning \cite{pineau2021improving}.
For a fuzzy regression, a mismatch between the training and inference partitions changes the design matrix after the coefficients have been fitted.
We therefore treat membership-parameter persistence as an explicit implementation invariant and report its failure mode separately from the corrected benchmark.

\section{Methodology}
\label{sec:method}

\subsection{TSK Fuzzy System}
\label{sec:tsk}

For input $\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^d$ and scalar target $y$, a TSK system with $R$ rules is
\begin{equation}
 y = \sum_{j=1}^{R} w_j(\mathbf{x})\,\big(\beta_{j0}+\beta_{j1}x_1+\cdots+\beta_{jd}x_d\big) + \varepsilon,\quad
 \varepsilon\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma^2),
 \label{eq:tsk}
\end{equation}
where $w_j(\mathbf{x})=\prod_{i=1}^{d}\mu_{A_{ji}}(x_i)\big/\sum_{k=1}^{R}\prod_{i=1}^{d}\mu_{A_{ki}}(x_i)$ is the normalized firing strength of rule $j$, and $A_{ji}$ are Gaussian antecedent fuzzy sets.
\begin{equation}
 \mu_{A_{ji}}(x_i)=\exp\!\Big(-\tfrac{(x_i-c_{ji})^2}{2 s_{ji}^2}\Big),
 \label{eq:membership}
\end{equation}
Centers $c_{ji}$ are obtained from fuzzy $c$-means clustering \cite{bezdek1984fcm}, and spreads $s_{ji}$ are set to the standard deviation of the training points assigned to each cluster, scaled by a factor $s$. Writing the weighted design matrix $\boldsymbol{\Phi}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times R(d+1)}$ and stacking consequents into $\boldsymbol{\beta}\in\mathbb{R}^{R(d+1)}$, Eq.~\eqref{eq:tsk} becomes the linear model $\mathbf{y}=\boldsymbol{\Phi}\boldsymbol{\beta}+\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}$.

\subsection{The Membership-Spread Bug}
\label{sec:bug}

The spreads $s_{ji}$ in Eq.~\eqref{eq:membership} define the fuzzy partition and must be identical at training and inference time.
A defect we audit recomputes them at prediction by passing query inputs and placeholder cluster labels to the membership routine.
The resulting partition no longer matches the one used to fit $\boldsymbol{\beta}$ and can produce numerically unstable predictions.
Because this defective path is a failure-mode reproduction rather than a valid benchmark, its output is not used as a headline effect size.
The corrected procedure stores the training-time centers and spreads and reuses them verbatim at inference, as in Algorithm~\ref{alg:tsk}.

\begin{algorithm}[tbhp]
\caption{Corrected TSK training and inference}
\label{alg:tsk}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require Training data $\{\mathbf{x}_i,y_i\}_{i=1}^n$, rule count $R$
\State $(\mathbf{c},\mathbf{u}) \gets \mathrm{FCM}(\mathbf{X}, R)$ \Comment{fuzzy $c$-means}
\State $\mathbf{s} \gets \{\text{per-cluster standard deviations from training points, scaled by } s\}$
\State Store $(\mathbf{c},\mathbf{s})$ \Comment{\emph{fix the partition at training time}}
\State Build $\boldsymbol{\Phi}$ via Eqs.~\eqref{eq:tsk}--\eqref{eq:membership} using $(\mathbf{c},\mathbf{s})$
\State Fit $\boldsymbol{\beta}$ (least squares, conjugate Bayes, or Gibbs; Sections~\ref{sec:conj}--\ref{sec:gibbs})
\State \Return model with frozen $(\mathbf{c},\mathbf{s},\boldsymbol{\beta})$
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\subsection{Conjugate Bayesian Baseline}
\label{sec:conj}

The dense Bayesian TSK places the scaled conjugate Gaussian prior $\boldsymbol{\beta}\mid\sigma^2\sim\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0},\sigma^2\tau^2\mathbf{I})$ and an inverse-gamma prior $\sigma^2\sim\mathrm{InvGamma}(0.01,0.01)$ on the noise variance. The posterior is available in closed form: with $\mathbf{P}=\boldsymbol{\Phi}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\Phi}+\tau^{-2}\mathbf{I}$,
\begin{equation}
 \boldsymbol{\beta}\mid\mathcal{D},\sigma^2 \sim \mathcal{N}\big(\mathbf{P}^{-1}\boldsymbol{\Phi}^{\top}\mathbf{y},\ \sigma^2\mathbf{P}^{-1}\big),
\end{equation}
and the predictive variance conditional on $\sigma^2$ at a new design row $\boldsymbol{\phi}^*$ is $\sigma^2 + \boldsymbol{\phi}^*(\sigma^2\mathbf{P}^{-1})\boldsymbol{\phi}^{*\top}$.
After integrating $\sigma^2$, the implementation reports the corresponding Student-$t$ posterior-predictive quantiles.
This closed-form model is a dense reference and a correctness target for the Gibbs updates when all rules are forced active.
The forced-active check is complemented by a small-$R$ enumeration check of the rule-inclusion update; both are reported in the Supplementary Material.
The weighted TSK design can be ill-conditioned, so the implementation uses symmetric solves, explicit diagonal stabilization, and no additional regression intercept after the rule-weighted intercept columns have been constructed.
The full condition-number and numerical-stability descriptors are retained in the phase-diagram output rather than summarized by a single benchmark-wide condition number.

\subsection{Spike-and-Slab Prior and Gibbs Sampling}
\label{sec:gibbs}

We decompose $\boldsymbol{\beta}$ into $R$ blocks $\boldsymbol{\beta}_j\in\mathbb{R}^{d+1}$ and place a spike-and-slab prior on each block:
\begin{equation}
 \boldsymbol{\beta}_j \mid \gamma_j,\sigma^2,\tau^2 \sim
 \begin{cases}
  \mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0},\sigma^2\tau^2\mathbf{I}_{d+1}), & \gamma_j=1 \ \ \text{(slab)},\\[3pt]
  \delta_{\mathbf{0}}, & \gamma_j=0 \ \ \text{(spike)},
 \end{cases}
 \label{eq:spikeslab}
\end{equation}
with $\gamma_j\sim\mathrm{Bernoulli}(\pi)$. We update $\boldsymbol{\gamma}$ with a block-Gibbs sampler rather than with the BIC criterion used by the comparator.
The sampler targets the specified posterior conditionally on the finite Monte Carlo run; its estimates therefore retain Monte Carlo error, which is quantified with the convergence diagnostics of Section~\ref{sec:setup}.
For block $j$, let $\mathbf{r}_{-j}=\mathbf{y}-\sum_{k\neq j}\boldsymbol{\Phi}_k\boldsymbol{\beta}_k$ be the residual excluding rule $j$, and let $\mathbf{A}_j=\boldsymbol{\Phi}_j^{\top}\boldsymbol{\Phi}_j$.
The marginal likelihood of $\mathbf{r}_{-j}$ under $\gamma_j=1$ (integrating out $\boldsymbol{\beta}_j$) is Gaussian with covariance $\sigma^2(\mathbf{I}+\tau^2\boldsymbol{\Phi}_j\boldsymbol{\Phi}_j^{\top})$.
Its determinant and quadratic form are computed in $\mathcal{O}(n(d+1)^2)$ via the matrix-determinant and Woodbury identities. The full conditional for $\gamma_j$ is
\begin{equation}
 P(\gamma_j=1\mid\cdot)=\sigma\!\Big(\log\tfrac{\pi}{1-\pi}
  -\tfrac12\log\det(\mathbf{I}+\tau^2\mathbf{A}_j)
  +\tfrac{\tau^2}{2\sigma^2}\mathbf{z}_j^{\top}(\mathbf{I}+\tau^2\mathbf{A}_j)^{-1}\mathbf{z}_j\Big),
 \label{eq:gamma}
\end{equation}
where $\mathbf{z}_j=\boldsymbol{\Phi}_j^{\top}\mathbf{r}_{-j}$ and $\sigma(\cdot)$ is the logistic function.
Conditional on $\gamma_j=1$, we draw $\boldsymbol{\beta}_j\sim\mathcal{N}\big((\mathbf{A}_j+\tau^{-2}\mathbf{I})^{-1}\mathbf{z}_j,\ \sigma^2(\mathbf{A}_j+\tau^{-2}\mathbf{I})^{-1}\big)$; otherwise $\boldsymbol{\beta}_j=\mathbf{0}$.
Finally $\sigma^2$ is drawn from its inverse-gamma full conditional with the corresponding active-block prior quadratic form.
A coefficient-level variant applies the same prior independently to each scalar coefficient $\beta_i$ (stochastic search variable selection, SSVS \cite{george1993variable}).
For numerical stability, the implementation uses a small diagonal jitter in sampled precision matrices, initializes coefficients from a ridge-regularized least-squares fit with no additional intercept, and floors the sampled noise variance at $10^{-4}$.

\subsection{Bayesian Model Averaging for Prediction}
\label{sec:bma}

We predict by BMA rather than by conditioning on a single selected model \cite{hoeting1999bayesian}; recent reviews of BMA practice and its pitfalls are given in \cite{fragoso2018bayesian}. Given $T$ posterior draws $\{\boldsymbol{\beta}^{(t)},\sigma^{2(t)}\}_{t=1}^{T}$, the predictive mean is the mixture mean $\hat{y}^*=\frac1T\sum_t \boldsymbol{\phi}^*\boldsymbol{\beta}^{(t)}$, and the predictive variance follows from the law of total variance:
\begin{equation}
 \mathrm{Var}(y^*\mid\mathbf{x}^*,\mathcal{D})
 = \underbrace{\tfrac1T\sum_t \sigma^{2(t)}}_{\text{aleatoric}}
 + \underbrace{\mathrm{Var}_t\big(\boldsymbol{\phi}^*\boldsymbol{\beta}^{(t)}\big)}_{\text{model uncertainty}},
 \label{eq:bma}
\end{equation}
The reported bounds are the direct 2.5th and 97.5th quantiles of this posterior-predictive mixture rather than moment-matched Gaussian bounds.
Over posterior draws the second term spans both between-model (rule-configuration) variation and within-model parameter variation.
This construction retains model-selection uncertainty; whether it improves calibration is an empirical question evaluated below.

\section{Experimental Setup}
\label{sec:setup}

We evaluate on two UCI regression benchmarks (three targets): the Energy Efficiency dataset \cite{tsanas2012accurate} (768 simulated building configurations, 8 geometric features, with heating and cooling loads as two targets) and the Concrete Compressive Strength dataset \cite{yeh1998modeling} (1030 laboratory samples, 8 mix-design features). Features are standardized to zero mean and unit variance; all TSK methods use $R=5$ rules from fuzzy $c$-means ($m=2$) with Gaussian membership spreads equal to the per-cluster standard deviation of training points, scaled by $s=1.5$.
A rule-count sweep ($R=3$--$10$, Supplementary) shows dense accuracy is nearly unchanged from $R=4$ upward on the Energy targets and improves only marginally beyond $R\approx7$ on Concrete, supporting the default $R=5$.

Four TSK methods are compared in the main experiments: \textbf{TSK-LS} (dense least squares), \textbf{Bayesian-TSK} (dense conjugate posterior, Section~\ref{sec:conj}), \textbf{TSK-SpikeSlab-BIC} (the BIC-plus-Laplace comparator with hard thresholding), and \textbf{TSK-SpikeSlab-Gibbs} (rule-level Gibbs plus BMA, Sections~\ref{sec:gibbs}--\ref{sec:bma}).
A coefficient-level variant, \textbf{TSK-SSVS}, is used in the sparsity analysis (Section~\ref{sec:sparse}). Random forest, support vector regression, and Gaussian-process regression serve as machine-learning references; the Gaussian process provides a probabilistic interval baseline.
Reference implementations use the scikit-learn configurations recorded in the experiment code.
A random forest with 300 trees and maximum depth 10, support vector regression with an RBF kernel ($C=1$, $\gamma=$ \texttt{scale}), and a Gaussian process with an RBF-plus-white-noise kernel, hyperparameters optimized by marginal likelihood (\texttt{normalize\_y=True}).
Fuzzy-specific interval-construction methods such as ENNreg \cite{denoeux2023ennreg} and interval-ANFIS \cite{miskony2018predictionintervals} build intervals through evidential combination or interval-network training rather than posterior model selection.
They are complementary to the present comparison. The rule-level Gibbs sampler uses $1{,}000$ burn-in and $2{,}000$ retained draws; the coefficient-level SSVS uses $800$ burn-in and $800$ retained draws.
For the rule-level sampler, three chains with independent seeds are used on representative splits.
The diagnostics show raw coefficient \(\hat R_{\max}=1.061\) for Energy-Cooling and \(1.030\) for Concrete, while the maximum predictive-quantity \(\hat R\) is \(1.004\) and \(1.027\), respectively; the mean ESS for \(\sigma^2\) is \(1{,}639.5\) and \(1{,}740.3\) (Supplementary Table~10).
These diagnostics do not establish perfect mixing, and no formal convergence claim is made for coefficient-level SSVS.
All results are averaged over 30 independent random 80/20 train--test splits with a fixed seed (SEED=42).
Bayesian intervals use direct 2.5th and 97.5th posterior-predictive quantiles; conformal intervals use a calibration split within each outer training set.
All spike-and-slab priors use inclusion probability $\pi=0.5$; the rule-level Gibbs and conjugate baselines use slab variance $\tau^2=10^3$, and the coefficient-level SSVS uses $\tau^2=1.0$.
Metrics are root-mean-square error (RMSE), $R^2$, prediction-interval coverage probability (PICP), mean prediction-interval width (MPIW), weighted interval score (WIS), and continuous ranked probability score (CRPS) when a predictive distribution is available.

\section{Results}
\label{sec:results}

\subsection{Implementation Audit}
\label{sec:repro}

The implementation audit confirmed that membership spreads must be persisted from training to inference.
When the defective query-time recomputation path was reproduced, the resulting predictions were numerically unstable, including an extreme negative $R^2$ on Energy-Cooling in the recorded defect run.
That output is retained as a failure-mode diagnostic, not presented as a corrected-versus-buggy performance estimate.
The main comparison below uses only the frozen-partition implementation, so its values can be traced to one stable protocol.

\subsection{Main Comparison}
\label{sec:main}

Table~\ref{tab:main} reports the primary results generated from the rebuilt JSON output.
Corrected TSK-LS gives $R^2=0.957$, $0.923$, and $0.786$ on Energy-Heating, Energy-Cooling, and Concrete.
The corresponding values for Bayesian-TSK are $0.957$, $0.923$, and $0.786$, and for TSK-SpikeSlab-Gibbs they are $0.957$, $0.922$, and $0.784$.
Thus, in this protocol, rule-level sampling with BMA is close to the dense and conjugate TSK references but does not demonstrate a predictive-accuracy gain.
The BIC comparator also remains close to the dense reference ($R^2=0.956$, $0.922$, and $0.786$); the negative-$R^2$ collapse from the earlier implementation is not reproduced after the correction.
The non-fuzzy references are stronger on point prediction for these data: the Gaussian process reaches $R^2=0.998$, $0.979$, and $0.889$, while random forest reaches $0.998$, $0.969$, and $0.903$.

\subsection{The Sparsity Boundary}
\label{sec:sparse}

We next examine whether the sparsity component changes the result.
In the 30-split main experiment, the rule-level Gibbs model retains a mean active-rule ratio of $0.713$, $0.700$, and $0.800$ on Heating, Cooling, and Concrete.
This is substantial selection, but it does not improve prediction: its $R^2$ is slightly below TSK-LS on all three targets.
The result is therefore not that the posterior is always fully active; rather, the selected rule configurations do not yield a stable accuracy gain in these data.

The coefficient-level sensitivity is shown in Fig.~\ref{fig:sparse} and Supplementary Table~6.
On the 10-split Energy-Cooling ablation, $R^2$ rises from $0.894$ at $\tau^2=0.1$ to $0.931$ at $\tau^2=100$.
The dense 10-split reference at the same protocol is $R^2=0.932$ (the zero-noise row in Supplementary Table~7), so the diffuse slab approaches but does not exceed the reference.
Appending standard-normal noise features changes the dense TSK-LS result from $R^2=0.932$ at zero appended features to $0.875$ at 30; the sparse variants remain close, with no consistent improvement across noise levels.
These observations support a protocol-bounded conclusion rather than a universal sparsity boundary.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

\subsection{What the Rebuild Changes}

The rebuilt evidence does not support a single-cause explanation for the negative results in the earlier implementation.
Under the corrected membership, prior, and numerical-stability choices, all four ablation configurations remain close on Energy-Cooling: BIC-BMA gives $R^2=0.937$, BIC-PIP thresholding with Laplace prediction gives $0.934$, Gibbs-PIP thresholding gives $0.933$, and the full BIC comparator gives $0.931$ (Supplementary Table~5).
The small ordering difference is compatible with a modest benefit from averaging in this ablation, but it is not evidence that BMA universally dominates, and it cannot be used to attribute the earlier collapse to thresholding or covariance alone.
The implementation audit therefore matters as much as the method comparison: changing membership persistence, prior scaling, and numerical linear algebra changes the object being compared.

\subsection{Implications for Sparse TSK Research}

The sparse results are conditional on the data geometry and the prior scale.
Rule-level selection is active in the low-dimensional benchmark, but it is not a reliable source of predictive improvement.
In the real high-dimensional probe, the dense TSK-LS and dense Bayesian model obtain $R^2=0.820$ and $0.822$, whereas rule-level Gibbs obtains $0.764$.
Coefficient-level SSVS gives $0.813$ at $\tau^2=1$ and $0.818$ at $\tau^2=10$; the complete grid reaches $0.819$ at its most diffuse setting, below the corresponding dense reference of $0.821$.
These results show why sparsity should be reported with active-set and sensitivity diagnostics, but they do not identify a general threshold at which sparse selection becomes beneficial.
Alternative shrinkage priors, such as the continuous spike-and-slab LASSO \cite{rockova2018spikeslab} and the horseshoe \cite{carvalho2010horseshoe}, may behave differently under the collinearity of a TSK weighted design matrix.

\subsection{Limitations}

Several limitations scope the conclusions.
First, the main evaluation contains two UCI datasets, three targets, a fixed FCM antecedent structure, and five rules; the Superconductivity experiment is a 3,000-row probe rather than a broad high-dimensional study.
Second, the raw coefficient diagnostics show \(\hat R_{\max}>1.03\) on the representative Gibbs runs, so finite-chain uncertainty and possible mixing problems remain relevant even though predictive-quantity diagnostics are smaller.
Third, the conformal comparator uses an inner calibration split and is not protocol-equivalent to the posterior-predictive intervals.
Fourth, the study applies sparsity to consequents only; joint antecedent--consequent selection \cite{gu2018bayesian} and alternative shrinkage priors remain open.
Finally, the Energy targets are simulated building-energy outputs; measured, submetered energy data would provide a stronger application test.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

We rebuilt a Bayesian TSK regression pipeline with explicit membership persistence, scaled priors, stable linear algebra, block-Gibbs sampling, and posterior-predictive model averaging.
Across three UCI targets, Gibbs/BMA produced point accuracy close to the dense and conjugate TSK references and empirical 95\% coverage close to, but not exactly at, the nominal level.
The Gaussian process was stronger on both point accuracy and proper probabilistic scores for these benchmarks, while split conformal produced wider intervals with higher empirical coverage.
The rebuilt BIC comparator did not reproduce the earlier negative-$R^2$ or severe-undercoverage behavior.
The appropriate conclusion is therefore an implementation- and protocol-bounded one: rule-level spike-and-slab model averaging is reproducible and diagnostically useful, but its predictive advantage over dense TSK is not established by this study.
