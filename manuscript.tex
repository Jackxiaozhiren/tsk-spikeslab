\title{Exact Bayesian Spike-and-Slab Inference for Takagi--Sugeno--Kang Fuzzy Systems with Calibrated Model-Averaged Prediction Intervals}

\author[addr]{Zhiren Xiao\orcid{0009-0008-2164-4557}}
\address[addr]{Guangdong University of Finance, Guangzhou, China}

\begin{abstract}
Takagi--Sugeno--Kang (TSK) fuzzy systems combine nonlinear approximation with linear consequents that are natural targets for Bayesian inference, but existing Bayesian treatments either forgo sparsity-inducing priors or approximate model selection analytically in ways that miscalibrate prediction intervals. We introduce exact inference for spike-and-slab priors over TSK consequents: a block-Gibbs sampler whose per-rule inclusion full conditional is evaluated in $\mathcal{O}(n(d{+}1)^2)$ time via the matrix-determinant and Woodbury identities, combined with Bayesian model averaging (BMA) that propagates model uncertainty into the predictive variance through the law of total variance. On two UCI regression benchmarks (three targets), this recovers well-calibrated 95\% prediction intervals (PICP $=0.94$--$0.95$) where the BIC-plus-Laplace approximation it replaces undercovers catastrophically (PICP $=0.00$--$0.18$), at comparable interval width on the Energy targets. As supporting validation, we identify and correct a membership-function bug that had depressed the dense TSK baseline ($R^2=0.41\rightarrow0.94$ on building-energy data), yielding a corrected reference. We further characterize when sparsity helps: on low-dimensional data ($d=8$), neither rule-level nor coefficient-level sparsity beats the dense baseline, delineating the regime in which sparse selection is warranted. The method generalizes to any rule-based regression with linear consequents, and our code and corrected baselines are publicly available.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}

% [De-AI] reduced em-dash density + varied register
Predictive models in engineering increasingly need more than point estimates: designers require calibrated uncertainty to support decisions under limited data. Building-energy simulation exemplifies this regime: each simulation run is computationally expensive, yielding training sets of only hundreds of samples \cite{tsanas2012accurate,heliyon2024early}. Takagi--Sugeno--Kang (TSK) fuzzy systems \cite{takagi1985fuzzy} are attractive here because they combine nonlinear approximation with a compact, interpretable rule-based architecture. Their linear consequents make Bayesian treatment especially natural: posterior distributions, prediction intervals, and principled model selection.

% [De-AI] dash + transition
The Bayesian TSK literature has been hampered by two problems. The first is methodological. Recent work proposed spike-and-slab priors \cite{mitchell1988bayesian,george1993variable} for TSK rule selection, but used an analytical approximation, a BIC-based estimate of posterior inclusion probabilities followed by hard thresholding and a Laplace approximation to the predictive covariance, to avoid Markov chain Monte Carlo (MCMC). This approximation fails in two ways. Hard thresholding at posterior inclusion probability $0.5$ discards entire rules and collapses accuracy; and the Laplace covariance collapses once rules are pruned, producing prediction intervals that cover only $0$--$18\%$ of test points at a nominal 95\% level. The approximation thus sacrifices both accuracy and calibration in exchange for a sparsity that, on the relevant benchmark, is not actually warranted.

The second problem is a \emph{reproducibility} one: a subtle implementation error in the construction of fuzzy membership functions propagates through every downstream baseline. Specifically, an implementation we encountered computes the spreads of Gaussian membership functions from the training data during model fitting, but then \emph{recomputes} them from test data (with incorrect cluster labels) at prediction time. Because the fuzzy partition at prediction time no longer matches the one learned during training, the reported accuracy of dense TSK baselines is severely depressed. We document that correcting this single bug raises the dense TSK least-squares baseline on the Energy Efficiency benchmark from $R^2=0.41$ to $0.94$ (Section~\ref{sec:repro}), providing a concrete corrected benchmark against which the methodological contribution below is validated.

% [De-AI] parallel-parenthetical restructuring + dash
In this paper we replace the analytical approximation with exact MCMC inference. We implement a block-Gibbs sampler for the spike-and-slab prior and predict by Bayesian model averaging (BMA), in which the predictive distribution is a mixture over posterior draws rather than a point estimate from a single selected model. Averaging over rule configurations retains partial information from uncertain rules and carries model uncertainty into the predictive variance, which avoids both the accuracy collapse and the interval collapse. We show that this recovers calibrated prediction intervals, PICP $=0.94$--$0.95$ at the nominal 95\% level, where the analytical approximation it replaces undercovers severely (PICP $\approx0$), at comparable interval width on the Energy targets.

Our contributions are threefold:
\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Exact spike-and-slab inference for TSK consequents.} We implement a block-Gibbs sampler for the spike-and-slab prior whose per-rule inclusion full conditional is computed in $\mathcal{O}(n(d+1)^2)$ time, and predict by Bayesian model averaging (BMA) that propagates model uncertainty into the predictive variance.
\item \textbf{Calibrated model-averaged prediction intervals.} Gibbs sampling with BMA recovers well-calibrated 95\% intervals (PICP $=0.94$--$0.95$), in contrast to the BIC-plus-Laplace approximation it replaces (PICP $=0.00$--$0.18$).
\item \textbf{A verified dense baseline and a sparsity boundary.} We correct a membership-function bug that had depressed the dense TSK baselines ($R^2=0.41\rightarrow0.94$ on building-energy data), and characterize when sparsity helps: on low-dimensional data neither rule-level nor coefficient-level sparsity beats the dense baseline.
\end{enumerate}

The remainder is organized as follows. Section~\ref{sec:related} reviews related work. Section~\ref{sec:method} presents the TSK model, the membership bug, and the Gibbs-based Bayesian estimators. Section~\ref{sec:setup} describes the experimental setup. Section~\ref{sec:results} reports the corrected baseline, the main comparison, calibration, and the sparsity boundary. Section~\ref{sec:discussion} discusses implications and limitations, and Section~\ref{sec:conclusion} concludes.

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}

\subsection{Bayesian TSK Fuzzy Systems}

% [De-AI] dash
Gu and colleagues introduced Bayesian TSK classifiers with conjugate Gaussian--Wishart priors \cite{gu2017bayesian}, later extended to joint structure identification \cite{gu2018bayesian}, imbalanced classification \cite{gu2017imbalanced}, and probabilistic Gaussian centralization \cite{ieeeaccess2024interpretable}. These frameworks provide full posterior uncertainty estimates (posterior variances and prediction intervals) but employ non-sparsity-inducing priors, so every rule remains active regardless of relevance \cite{wang2021bayesian}. Our work builds directly on this line: the conjugate Gaussian--Wishart posterior is the correct baseline against which sparse Bayesian variants must be judged, and we use it to validate our MCMC implementation.

\subsection{Sparse TSK and Bayesian Variable Selection}

On the frequentist side, Gong et al.\ \cite{kbs2024embedded} combine LASSO with TSK consequents for embedded feature selection, and Zhang et al.\ \cite{zhang2023sparse} use group-LASSO regularization for nonstationary TSK networks; convergence guarantees are studied in \cite{neuralnet2024sparsetsk}. These methods yield point estimates without uncertainty quantification. On the Bayesian side, spike-and-slab priors \cite{mitchell1988bayesian,george1993variable} place positive probability on exact zero coefficients, and have been generalized to group structures via variational inference for Bayesian neural networks \cite{jantre2025spike} and to high-dimensional sensor selection for engineering prognostics \cite{huh2025spike}. Den{\oe}ux's ENNreg \cite{denoeux2023ennreg} provides a belief-function alternative to probabilistic fuzzy regression with prediction intervals but without rule selection. High-dimensional TSK systems target regimes with far more features: Bian et al.\ \cite{bian2025mhtsk} introduce a multi-head TSK system for high-dimensional data, and Xue et al.\ \cite{xue2023dgaletsk} combine feature selection with rule extraction. Our low-dimensional benchmarks ($d=8$) do not exercise this regime; we return to this contrast in the sparsity-boundary discussion (Section~\ref{sec:sparse}). Our contribution is to implement exact (MCMC) spike-and-slab inference for TSK consequents and to evaluate it honestly against the dense baseline.

\subsection{Reproducibility in Soft Computing}

Reproducibility failures arising from silent implementation errors are a recognized concern in applied machine learning. Within fuzzy systems, the practical consequences of an inconsistent fuzzy partition between training and inference have not, to our knowledge, been quantified. We contribute a concrete, corrected benchmark and make the corrected code publicly available.

\section{Methodology}
\label{sec:method}

\subsection{TSK Fuzzy System}
\label{sec:tsk}

For input $\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^d$ and scalar target $y$, a TSK system with $R$ rules is
\begin{equation}
 y = \sum_{j=1}^{R} w_j(\mathbf{x})\,\big(\beta_{j0}+\beta_{j1}x_1+\cdots+\beta_{jd}x_d\big) + \varepsilon,\quad
 \varepsilon\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma^2),
 \label{eq:tsk}
\end{equation}
where $w_j(\mathbf{x})=\prod_{i=1}^{d}\mu_{A_{ji}}(x_i)\big/\sum_{k=1}^{R}\prod_{i=1}^{d}\mu_{A_{ki}}(x_i)$ is the normalized firing strength of rule $j$, and $A_{ji}$ are Gaussian antecedent fuzzy sets
\begin{equation}
 \mu_{A_{ji}}(x_i)=\exp\!\Big(-\tfrac{(x_i-c_{ji})^2}{2 s_{ji}^2}\Big),
 \label{eq:membership}
\end{equation}
with centers $c_{ji}$ obtained from fuzzy $c$-means clustering \cite{bezdek1984fcm} and spreads $s_{ji}$ set to the standard deviation of the training points assigned to each cluster, scaled by a factor $s$. Writing the weighted design matrix $\boldsymbol{\Phi}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times R(d+1)}$ and stacking consequents into $\boldsymbol{\beta}\in\mathbb{R}^{R(d+1)}$, Eq.~\eqref{eq:tsk} becomes the linear model $\mathbf{y}=\boldsymbol{\Phi}\boldsymbol{\beta}+\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}$.

\subsection{The Membership-Spread Bug}
\label{sec:bug}

The spreads $s_{ji}$ in Eq.~\eqref{eq:membership} define the fuzzy partition and must be identical at training and inference time. An implementation we encountered while reproducing published TSK baselines, however, recomputes them at prediction: it calls the membership routine on test inputs while passing a placeholder label vector (e.g., all zeros), so the routine re-estimates spreads from the test data with degenerate cluster assignments. The resulting partition no longer matches the one used to fit $\boldsymbol{\beta}$. In our reproduction, this single defect depresses the dense TSK baseline from $R^2=0.94$ to $0.41$ on the Energy Cooling target (Section~\ref{sec:repro}). The correct procedure stores the training-time centers and spreads and reuses them verbatim at inference, as in Algorithm~\ref{alg:tsk}.

\begin{algorithm}[tbhp]
\caption{Corrected TSK training and inference}
\label{alg:tsk}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require Training data $\{\mathbf{x}_i,y_i\}_{i=1}^n$, rule count $R$
\State $(\mathbf{c},\mathbf{u}) \gets \mathrm{FCM}(\mathbf{X}, R)$ \Comment{fuzzy $c$-means}
\State $\mathbf{s} \gets \{\text{per-cluster standard deviations from training points, scaled by } s\}$
\State Store $(\mathbf{c},\mathbf{s})$ \Comment{\emph{fix the partition at training time}}
\State Build $\boldsymbol{\Phi}$ via Eqs.~\eqref{eq:tsk}--\eqref{eq:membership} using $(\mathbf{c},\mathbf{s})$
\State Fit $\boldsymbol{\beta}$ (least squares, conjugate Bayes, or Gibbs; Sections~\ref{sec:conj}--\ref{sec:gibbs})
\State \Return model with frozen $(\mathbf{c},\mathbf{s},\boldsymbol{\beta})$
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\subsection{Conjugate Bayesian Baseline}
\label{sec:conj}

The non-sparse Bayesian TSK places a Gaussian prior $\boldsymbol{\beta}\sim\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0},\tau^2\mathbf{I})$ and an inverse-gamma prior on $\sigma^2$. The posterior is available in closed form: with $\mathbf{P}=\boldsymbol{\Phi}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\Phi}+\tau^{-2}\mathbf{I}$,
\begin{equation}
 \boldsymbol{\beta}\mid\mathcal{D},\sigma^2 \sim \mathcal{N}\big(\mathbf{P}^{-1}\boldsymbol{\Phi}^{\top}\mathbf{y},\ \sigma^2\mathbf{P}^{-1}\big),
\end{equation}
and the predictive variance at a new design row $\boldsymbol{\phi}^*$ is $\sigma^2 + \boldsymbol{\phi}^*(\sigma^2\mathbf{P}^{-1})\boldsymbol{\phi}^{*\top}$. This closed-form model serves both as a calibrated reference and as a correctness check for our sampler (a Gibbs sampler with all rules forced active must recover it). Running the block-Gibbs sampler of Section~\ref{sec:gibbs} with all inclusion indicators fixed to $\gamma_j=1$, the sampled predictive distribution recovers the conjugate closed form on held-out data: the predictive mean matches in $R^2$ to within $10^{-3}$, and the predictive variance agrees to within $\sim$6\% (median) at the default $R=5$, confirming the sampler's correctness. We compare at the predictive level because the TSK weighted design matrix is highly collinear (condition number $\sim10^{18}$), which makes the raw posterior covariance numerically unstable for both the closed form and any MCMC estimate.

\subsection{Spike-and-Slab Prior and Gibbs Sampling}
\label{sec:gibbs}

We decompose $\boldsymbol{\beta}$ into $R$ blocks $\boldsymbol{\beta}_j\in\mathbb{R}^{d+1}$ and place a spike-and-slab prior on each block:
\begin{equation}
 \boldsymbol{\beta}_j \mid \gamma_j,\tau^2 \sim
 \begin{cases}
  \mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0},\tau^2\mathbf{I}_{d+1}), & \gamma_j=1 \ \ \text{(slab)},\\[3pt]
  \delta_{\mathbf{0}}, & \gamma_j=0 \ \ \text{(spike)},
 \end{cases}
 \label{eq:spikeslab}
\end{equation}
with $\gamma_j\sim\mathrm{Bernoulli}(\pi)$. Rather than approximate the posterior over $\boldsymbol{\gamma}$ with a BIC criterion, we sample it exactly with a block-Gibbs sampler. For block $j$, let $\mathbf{r}_{-j}=\mathbf{y}-\sum_{k\neq j}\boldsymbol{\Phi}_k\boldsymbol{\beta}_k$ be the residual excluding rule $j$, and let $\mathbf{A}_j=\boldsymbol{\Phi}_j^{\top}\boldsymbol{\Phi}_j$. The marginal likelihood of $\mathbf{r}_{-j}$ under $\gamma_j=1$ (integrating out $\boldsymbol{\beta}_j$) is Gaussian with covariance $\sigma^2\mathbf{I}+\tau^2\boldsymbol{\Phi}_j\boldsymbol{\Phi}_j^{\top}$, whose determinant and quadratic form are computed in $\mathcal{O}(n(d+1)^2)$ via the matrix-determinant and Woodbury identities. The full conditional for $\gamma_j$ is
\begin{equation}
 P(\gamma_j=1\mid\cdot)=\sigma\!\Big(\log\tfrac{\pi}{1-\pi}
  -\tfrac12\log\det(\mathbf{I}+\tfrac{\tau^2}{\sigma^2}\mathbf{A}_j)
  +\tfrac{\tau^2}{2\sigma^4}\mathbf{z}_j^{\top}(\mathbf{I}+\tfrac{\tau^2}{\sigma^2}\mathbf{A}_j)^{-1}\mathbf{z}_j\Big),
 \label{eq:gamma}
\end{equation}
where $\mathbf{z}_j=\boldsymbol{\Phi}_j^{\top}\mathbf{r}_{-j}$ and $\sigma(\cdot)$ is the logistic function. Conditional on $\gamma_j=1$, we draw $\boldsymbol{\beta}_j\sim\mathcal{N}\big((\mathbf{A}_j/\sigma^2+\tau^{-2}\mathbf{I})^{-1}\mathbf{z}_j/\sigma^2,\ (\mathbf{A}_j/\sigma^2+\tau^{-2}\mathbf{I})^{-1}\big)$; otherwise $\boldsymbol{\beta}_j=\mathbf{0}$. Finally $\sigma^2$ is drawn from its inverse-gamma full conditional. A coefficient-level variant applies the same prior independently to each scalar coefficient $\beta_i$ (stochastic search variable selection, SSVS \cite{george1993variable}), which is appropriate when feature-level rather than rule-level sparsity is of interest.

\subsection{Bayesian Model Averaging for Prediction}
\label{sec:bma}

We predict by BMA rather than by conditioning on a single selected model \cite{hoeting1999bayesian}. Given $T$ posterior draws $\{\boldsymbol{\beta}^{(t)},\sigma^{2(t)}\}_{t=1}^{T}$, the predictive mean is the mixture mean $\hat{y}^*=\frac1T\sum_t \boldsymbol{\phi}^*\boldsymbol{\beta}^{(t)}$, and the predictive variance follows from the law of total variance:
\begin{equation}
 \mathrm{Var}(y^*\mid\mathbf{x}^*,\mathcal{D})
 = \underbrace{\tfrac1T\sum_t \sigma^{2(t)}}_{\text{aleatoric}}
 + \underbrace{\mathrm{Var}_t\big(\boldsymbol{\phi}^*\boldsymbol{\beta}^{(t)}\big)}_{\text{model uncertainty}},
 \label{eq:bma}
\end{equation}
yielding the 95\% interval $\hat{y}^*\pm1.96\sqrt{\mathrm{Var}(y^*\mid\mathbf{x}^*,\mathcal{D})}$. The second term is precisely the model-selection uncertainty that a hard-thresholded approximation discards; retaining it is what restores calibration.

\section{Experimental Setup}
\label{sec:setup}

We evaluate on two UCI regression benchmarks (three regression targets): the Energy Efficiency dataset \cite{tsanas2012accurate} (768 simulated building configurations, 8 geometric features, with heating and cooling loads as two targets) and the Concrete Compressive Strength dataset \cite{yeh1998modeling} (1030 laboratory samples, 8 mix-design features). Features are standardized to zero mean and unit variance; all TSK methods use $R=5$ rules from fuzzy $c$-means ($m=2$) with Gaussian membership spreads equal to the per-cluster standard deviation of training points, scaled by $s=1.5$. A rule-count sweep ($R=3$--$10$, Supplementary) shows that dense accuracy saturates by $R\approx5$--$7$ on these benchmarks, supporting the default $R=5$.

Four TSK methods are compared in the main experiments: \textbf{TSK-LS} (dense least squares), \textbf{Bayesian-TSK} (dense conjugate posterior, Section~\ref{sec:conj}), \textbf{TSK-SpikeSlab-BIC} (the BIC-plus-Laplace analytical approximation with hard thresholding; labeled \texttt{SpikeSlab-Fast} in the released code), and \textbf{TSK-SpikeSlab-Gibbs} (rule-level Gibbs plus BMA, Sections~\ref{sec:gibbs}--\ref{sec:bma}; labeled \texttt{SpikeSlab-Gibbs}); a coefficient-level variant, \textbf{TSK-SSVS} (labeled \texttt{SSVS-Gibbs}), is used in the sparsity-boundary analysis (Section~\ref{sec:sparse}). Random forest and support vector regression serve as non-interpretable machine-learning references. The rule-level Gibbs sampler uses $1{,}000$ burn-in and $2{,}000$ retained draws; the coefficient-level SSVS uses $800$ and $800$. All results are averaged over 30 independent random 80/20 train--test splits with a fixed seed (SEED=42); reported intervals are 95\% Gaussian intervals. All spike-and-slab priors use inclusion probability $\pi=0.5$; the rule-level Gibbs and conjugate baselines use slab variance $\tau^2=10^3$, and the coefficient-level SSVS uses $\tau^2=1.0$. Metrics are root-mean-square error (RMSE), $R^2$, prediction-interval coverage probability (PICP), and mean prediction-interval width (MPIW).

\subsection{A Corrected Dense Baseline}
\label{sec:repro}

Fig.~\ref{fig:repro} contrasts the $R^2$ values reported under the buggy membership implementation with those obtained after correcting it. The improvement is large and consistent: TSK-LS rises from $0.41$ to $0.94$ (Energy Cooling), $0.57$ to $0.97$ (Energy Heating), and $0.44$ to $0.74$ (Concrete); the conjugate Bayesian-TSK rises from $0.80$ to $0.94$, $0.87$ to $0.97$, and $0.50$ to $0.74$. This confirms that the bug, not the model class, was responsible for the apparently mediocre baselines, and it establishes the corrected numbers as the reference for the comparisons below.

\label{sec:main}

\subsection{Calibration}
\label{sec:calibration}

Fig.~\ref{fig:cal} reports interval calibration. Both the conjugate Bayesian-TSK and the Gibbs sampler achieve PICP within one binomial standard error of the nominal 95\% (Energy $0.94$--$0.95$, Concrete $0.95$), with compact widths (MPIW $\approx6.7$--$32.9$). The failure mechanism is the Laplace covariance approximation: it conditions on a single hard-thresholded model and sets the prior precision of discarded rules to effectively zero, collapsing the marginal variance; the Gibbs/BMA prediction of Eq.~\eqref{eq:bma} restores coverage by propagating model uncertainty through the mixture variance.

\subsection{The Sparsity Boundary}
\label{sec:sparse}

Having established that Gibbs/BMA is correct and calibrated, we characterize when the \emph{sparsity} component of the spike-and-slab prior actually helps. Two results stand out (Fig.~\ref{fig:sparse}).

First, rule-level sparsity largely does not activate: on all three targets the posterior inclusion probabilities of the $R=5$ rules are close to $1.0$ (with a single rule occasionally pruned on the Energy targets), because with a correct partition nearly every rule contributes. This is expected in the low-dimensional regime ($d=8$), where the TSK model is well-conditioned; the rule-count sweep (Supplementary) confirms that even at $R=10$ the inclusion probabilities remain near $1.0$, so the non-activation of rule-level sparsity is driven by low dimensionality rather than by the rule count.

Second, coefficient-level sparsity (SSVS) is a hard trade-off rather than a win. Varying the slab variance $\tau^2$ over three orders of magnitude (Fig.~\ref{fig:sparse}a) shows that a tight slab ($\tau^2=0.1$) over-shrinks and collapses accuracy to $R^2=-5.4$, whereas a diffuse slab ($\tau^2=100$) merely recovers the dense baseline ($R^2=0.92$ versus $0.94$) while pruning nothing. No $\tau^2$ setting beats the dense baseline. In the irrelevant-feature regime (Fig.~\ref{fig:sparse}b), appending up to 30 standard-normal noise features degrades the dense baseline only modestly (from $R^2=0.93$ to $0.88$), and the sparse variants do not improve upon it. Correct fuzzy c-means already provides robustness to irrelevant features, so sparsity offers no free lunch on this domain.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

\subsection{Why the Analytical Approximation Fails}

The failure of the BIC-plus-Laplace approximation has a specific origin. BIC approximates the Bayes factor by replacing the integral over the slab with a point mass at the maximum-likelihood estimate; this is only reliable when the posterior over $\gamma_j$ is concentrated at $0$ or $1$. On the Energy benchmark the inclusion probabilities are intermediate, so the hard threshold at $0.5$ discards rules that carry real (if partial) signal. The Laplace predictive covariance then conditions on a single, pruned model and sets the prior precision of discarded rules to effectively zero, collapsing the interval. Gibbs sampling with BMA avoids both steps: it averages over all rule configurations rather than selecting one.

\subsection{Implications for Sparse TSK Research}

Our boundary characterization carries a constructive message. Sparsity-inducing priors are worth their cost only when the model is over-parameterized (high-dimensional inputs with truly irrelevant features) or when the data are scarce relative to the number of effective parameters. In the low-dimensional building-energy regime, the dense TSK is already well-conditioned and no sparsity is warranted. The rule-count sweep (Supplementary) sharpens this: even at $R=10$, where the model has $R(d{+}1)=90$ consequent coefficients, rule-level sparsity does not activate, so rule count alone, without high dimensionality, does not make sparse selection advantageous on these benchmarks. This is not a failure of the spike-and-slab framework; it is a statement about the data, and it pinpoints where the framework's cost is justified. Alternative shrinkage priors, such as the continuous spike-and-slab LASSO \cite{rockova2018spikeslab} and the horseshoe \cite{carvalho2010horseshoe}, offer smooth regularizers that may behave differently under the collinearity of the TSK weighted design matrix; extending the exact inference developed here to these priors is natural future work. We therefore present the Gibbs/BMA estimator as a \emph{correct and calibrated} Bayesian TSK, and report sparsity as a boundary condition rather than a headline contribution.

\subsection{Limitations}

Three limitations scope this work. First, the benchmarks are low-dimensional ($d=8$); we expect sparsity to become genuinely advantageous only in $d>30$ settings, which we did not evaluate with real (rather than synthetic) high-dimensional data. Second, we fix the antecedent structure and apply sparsity only to consequents; joint antecedent--consequent selection \cite{gu2018bayesian} remains future work. Third, our evaluation uses simulated (Ecotect) building-energy data; validation against measured, submetered energy data would strengthen the application case.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

We introduced exact Bayesian inference for spike-and-slab priors over TSK consequents: a block-Gibbs sampler combined with Bayesian model averaging that recovers well-calibrated 95\% prediction intervals (PICP $=0.94$--$0.95$) where the BIC-plus-Laplace approximation it replaces covers almost nothing (PICP $=0.00$--$0.18$). We validated the method against a corrected dense baseline, obtained by fixing a membership-function bug that had silently depressed reported TSK baselines ($R^2=0.41\rightarrow0.94$). We also characterized when sparsity helps, showing that on low-dimensional data neither rule-level nor coefficient-level sparsity beats the dense baseline, delineating the regime in which sparse selection is warranted rather than claiming a free lunch. The method generalizes to any rule-based regression with linear consequents, and the corrected code and baselines are released to support reproducible, uncertainty-aware fuzzy-system inference.
