\section{Slab Variance Sensitivity (Coefficient-Level SSVS)}

Table~\ref{tab:tau2} reports the coefficient-level SSVS ablation on the Energy-Cooling target (10 splits, $\pi=0.5$, $R=5$ rules). Varying the slab variance $\tau^2$ from $0.1$ to $100$ (three orders of magnitude) spans the full sparsity--accuracy trade-off: a tight slab ($\tau^2=0.1$) over-shrinks the coefficients and collapses accuracy to $R^2=-5.42$, whereas a diffuse slab ($\tau^2=100$) merely recovers the dense baseline ($R^2=0.92$ versus $0.94$ for TSK-LS) while pruning nothing. \section{Rule-Count Sensitivity}

Table~\ref{tab:rsweep} sweeps the rule count $R$ from $3$ to $10$ on all three targets (10 splits). Two conclusions follow. First, $R=5$ is a reasonable default: the dense (TSK-LS) and conjugate (Bayesian-TSK) accuracy saturates by $R\approx5$--$7$ on every target, and the Gibbs sampler tracks the dense baseline at every $R$, providing a consistency check of the sampler outside its default configuration. Second, the sparsity boundary is robust to the rule count: even at $R=10$ (an over-parameterized model with $R(d{+}1)=90$ consequent coefficients) the Gibbs sampler keeps nearly all rules active (mean $9.7/10$), so rule-level sparsity does not activate even when the model is heavily over-parameterized. The BIC-based approximation (TSK-SpikeSlab-BIC) collapses to negative $R^2$ at every rule count.

\section{Ablation Isolating the Failure of the BIC Baseline}

Table~\ref{tab:ablation} isolates which step of the BIC-plus-Laplace baseline causes its collapse on Energy-Cooling (10 splits, $R=5$). Four configurations are compared:

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{BIC weights + BMA} -- all $2^R$ rule subsets are weighted by $\exp(-\mathrm{BIC}/2)$ and averaged, so no hard threshold and no Laplace covariance are used;
\item \textbf{BIC-PIP + threshold + Laplace} -- the per-rule BIC inclusion probabilities are hard-thresholded at $0.5$, the selected model is fit by least squares, and the Laplace predictive covariance is used;
\item \textbf{Gibbs-PIP + threshold + Laplace} -- the same prediction as (2), but the inclusion probabilities come from the Gibbs sampler rather than BIC;
\item \textbf{Full BIC baseline} -- the as-implemented baseline of the main text, which additionally fits the selected model with ridge regularization.
\end{enumerate}

Three conclusions follow. First, averaging is the decisive change: BIC weights fed through BMA (row 1) restore dense accuracy and near-calibration ($R^2=0.95$, PICP $0.92$) even though the model probabilities still come from BIC, whereas hard-thresholding those same probabilities to a single model (row 2) clearly underperforms ($R^2=0.60$). Second, the Gibbs inclusion probabilities are reliable: hard-thresholding and Laplace prediction built on them (row 3) reach the dense baseline ($R^2=0.94$, PICP $0.94$). Third, the as-implemented baseline (row 4) collapses to $R^2=-2.5$, with part of the gap attributable to its ridge-regularized least-squares fit of the highly collinear TSK design matrix (condition number $\sim10^{18}$). Gibbs sampling with BMA is the only configuration that is simultaneously accurate and calibrated.

\section{MCMC Convergence Diagnostics}

Table~\ref{tab:mcmc} reports multi-chain convergence diagnostics for the rule-level Gibbs sampler (TSK-SpikeSlab-Gibbs) on a representative split of two targets. Three chains are run with independent seeds ($42$, $123$, $456$), each with $1{,}000$ burn-in and $2{,}000$ retained draws. The Gelman--Rubin statistic satisfies $\hat{R}\le1.01$ for the inclusion indicators ($\boldsymbol{\gamma}$), the consequents ($\boldsymbol{\beta}$), and the noise variance ($\sigma^2$), and the effective sample size equals the full chain length for $\boldsymbol{\gamma}$ and exceeds $1{,}700$ for $\sigma^2$, indicating well-mixed chains. On Concrete the inclusion indicators are deterministically active (posterior inclusion probability $=1.0$ for every rule), so the $\boldsymbol{\gamma}$ chain is constant; the $\boldsymbol{\beta}$ and $\sigma^2$ chains mix as above. Diagnostics are reported for the rule-level sampler; on these benchmarks the $\boldsymbol{\gamma}$ chains of the coefficient-level SSVS also saturate near full activation, so we expect qualitatively similar mixing, but we do not claim formal convergence verification for those runs.

\section{High-Dimensional Sparsity Boundary}

Table~\ref{tab:highdim} tests the sparsity boundary on a real high-dimensional regression benchmark (Superconductivity, UCI id 464; subsample of $n=3000$ rows with $d=81$ features, $30$ splits, $R=5$ rules). The pattern of the low-dimensional benchmarks reproduces almost exactly: rule-level sparsity (TSK-SpikeSlab-Gibbs) matches the dense baselines in $R^2$ ($0.794$ versus $0.792$--$0.795$) while keeping calibration at the nominal level (PICP $0.940$), and coefficient-level sparsity (TSK-SSVS) over-shrinks, with a tight slab ($\tau^2=1$) collapsing $R^2$ to $0.674$. A diffuse slab ($\tau^2=10$) recovers only to $0.763$, still below dense. The sparsity boundary therefore is not a low-dimensional artifact; whether sparsity pays off under different antecedent structures or much larger samples remains open. Notably, on this high-dimensional target the rule-level Gibbs sampler maintains nominal calibration (PICP $0.940$) where the dense conjugate baseline slightly undercovers (PICP $0.921$), consistent with the calibration contribution of the main text.
